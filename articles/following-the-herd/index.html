Following the herd

Don’t follow the logo, just use the right tool for the job.

Awkward timing

I recently joined a new company and switched back from native mobile to React Native. For a lot of the native modules that are used here, the work’s already been done. There isn’t really any need to touch the native code.

At my previous company I had moved from React Native to native, then continued in native iOS for close to two years. I’ve previously worked on native projects and helped migrate projects between native and cross-platform for various reasons.

So the switch didn’t bother me; it’s always refreshing to jump into a new codebase and learn new ways of working and the processes that funnel into them.

So why am I writing this? Well, Shopify recently announced they were moving away from React Native, which makes the timing a little awkward. For the React Native community this is a shocking announcement, as they are big contributors to the framework and supporting libraries.

Even more recently, Discord announced here that they’ve been working in Rust since 2018 and that a lot of their infra and tooling, and even some of the client, uses Rust. So they are migrating what’s left of the client to Rust, which in turn means no Electron or React Native.

What actually happened

So why the change all of a sudden? The easy answer is AI, at least for Shopify, and maybe for Discord too.

Shopify

Back in September 2026, a post was put out by Mustafa Ali, Director of Engineering and Head of Mobile at Shopify, about their decision to move from React Native to native. You can read the post here.

In the post he talks about why they originally decided to use React Native, which will resonate with a lot of people: “stop building the same features twice”, “allow developers to work across the stack”, “spend less time chasing feature parity and more time shipping value”. These are generally the fundamental reasons for choosing React Native: as a cross-platform framework, you build once and deploy everywhere. What’s not to love?

He continues with why they are choosing to go native: “What changed is that agents can now do enough of the implementation, translation, testing, and review work that it’s no longer the deciding factor it was.”

Simply put, AI and agents have now removed several barriers in the decision-making process. Engineers can now implement in their favoured language and ask AI to build the opposing platform. It makes it easier to build out new features even when you aren’t in your strongest language, and all the additional maintenance costs of supporting several platforms go away when you can lean on agents.

A key paragraph:

React Native apps can be fast. Ours are. We are making this change because agents have reduced the advantages of sharing implementation, while the advantages of building for each platform remain. Native keeps us closer to platform capabilities and first-party tooling, with fewer framework and dependency layers between our code and the platform.

They are making the decision based on years of implementation and evaluation, and that’s the key here: choosing a language or framework that supports your company’s needs and the team’s needs, as well as the trajectory you are pushing towards.

Discord

The announcement came in November 2026 via X (or Twitter, if you’re a holdout) from Discord’s CTO and co-founder, Stanislav Vishnevskiy.

Discord has used Rust since 2018, in its infrastructure, voice technology and parts of the current client, and the plan is to get the whole client onto it. That means retiring Electron on desktop and React Native on mobile. The new desktop UI is built on GPUI, a GPU-accelerated Rust UI framework that comes out of the team behind the Zed editor. On mobile, the UI stays native and the app code is shared with desktop through Rust.

That last part matters for this article. Discord isn’t swapping React Native for another cross-platform UI layer. It’s keeping native UI on mobile and sharing the logic underneath, which I’ll come back to in the hybrid section.

Discord hasn’t published a long list of reasons, so I can’t tell you exactly why they made the call. What I can tell you is why Rust keeps turning up in migrations like this, the “Rustification” as people have started calling it:

  • It enforces memory safety at compile time, which rules out whole classes of bugs. Security agencies have been recommending memory-safe languages for a while now.
  • It compiles to native code without a heavy runtime, so it tends to use less CPU and memory. Cloudflare reported significant savings after writing its Pingora proxy in Rust.
  • It’s already inside the tools you use. A lot of the tooling around Python and TypeScript is now written in Rust.
  • It suits AI infrastructure, where squeezing performance out of CPUs and GPUs matters and much of the tooling is written in Rust.

There’s one more possible link to AI, and it’s the same one Shopify gave. If agents lower the cost of a rewrite, and the compiler catches a good share of what an agent gets wrong, a rewrite looks a lot less scary. That’s my guess, though. Discord hasn’t said it.

The sheep problem

This is more commonly known as herd mentality and refers to the tendency of people to conform their behaviour or beliefs to those around them or the groups they belong to.

What this means is that when big organisations like Google, Meta, Anthropic and OpenAI make decisions, people listen. They watch their next move because it must be the right decision. There’s a social safety to having the decision made for you, so it’s easy to just go with the flow and be a sheep.

The group we belong to is the mobile ecosystem and the companies that make our lives easier by supporting and building the tools we use every day. So when a company like Shopify makes a decision like migrating, it makes people talk; it makes them stop and question whether they should be following.

The problem, I feel, is that everyone has different motives for making this kind of decision. Whether it’s the right one for your company depends on a multitude of factors, and the fact you have the ability to use AI to follow suit probably isn’t a good reason. Shopify has hundreds of engineers, a plethora of screens and apps, and its own agent tooling. They aren’t an indie developer or a small startup with a team of five trying to figure out the best architecture.

However, there are reasons to pay attention when things like this happen. If maintainers leave, the ecosystem risk is real. FlashList has around two million weekly downloads and Skia is being forked, so dependency stewardship matters even if the framework is fine.

The case for React Native

I like React Native (RN), and I have reasons beyond habit.

I’ve been involved in startups and small to medium apps where it was the obvious call. Usually my experience is either a small team of 2-4 mobile engineers working on the initial build of the mobile app, trying to deliver as many features as possible to give the most value, or several feature teams of around 4 engineers owning various products from start to finish. When you have small teams of engineers and a runway measured in months, building everything twice isn’t a trade-off. It’s a non-starter.

I’m also currently in a larger organisation working on a Super App, which is essentially many products funnelling into a larger codebase and core app. Again, think small teams: about 8 teams, each owning a product, with 2-6 engineers apiece. React Native still works and pays off here. There aren’t any huge native capabilities needed, and engineers tend to float across web, mobile, and backend or infra. So it makes sense in an organisation like ours.

The framework’s also moved on from what many of its critics remember. The old bridge serialised every message between JavaScript and native, and it caused a lot of the performance complaints. The New Architecture replaces it with direct communication, and it’s been the default for a while now. Much of the “RN is slow” reputation comes from that era.

Then there’s the new-OS problem. Apple or Google ships something on day one, it’s available in Swift and Kotlin immediately, and the cross-platform wrapper arrives weeks or months later, if ever. Native modules close that gap. You write the thin native layer yourself and expose it to JavaScript rather than waiting on a library maintainer. I once had to implement an SDK for Conviva streaming analytics, which didn’t have an RN integration. I’m fortunate to know both iOS and Android, so I quickly built a wrapper to expose the core functionality of the SDK, which took me a week. There are other examples where I’ve built whole features in native modules to support audio playback with native performance capabilities. That took months, as it was a whole feature in its own right and I had to write it twice. It’s a good escape hatch, but note that using it means someone on the team needs to know the native side.

It also has real costs, and Shopify listed them plainly in their migration post: performance tuning, keeping up with framework updates, and managing external dependencies. Anyone who has done a major version upgrade knows the feeling. I won’t talk about my upgrade stories, but I will add that the JavaScript ecosystem can come with a lot of dependencies that you have to resolve to even get the code building. Shopify accepted those costs for years because the benefits outweighed them, and I think that was the right call.

The case for native

I’ve also worked in codebases where native was chosen deliberately. When I asked why, the answer was usually some version of “it’s faster” or “it’s closer to the APIs”.

“Closer to the APIs” is true and worth having. You get new platform features on day one, first-party tooling like the view hierarchy debugger and profiling, and fewer layers between you and the platform when something breaks. Shopify makes a similar point about fewer framework and dependency layers.

“Faster” is where I get wary. A belief isn’t a benchmark. In a previous app, the decision was made to switch to native entirely based on the premise that RN was the root cause of the performance issues, when we had so many other integrations slowing network connections and startup times.

Most apps spend their time on lists, forms and network calls, and the UI layer is rarely the bottleneck. Where the app does something demanding, like heavy animation, camera pipelines or background processing, the gap can be real, and then it’s worth paying for. But that’s a measured reason, not an inherited one.

The price of native hasn’t changed: two codebases, two skill sets, and constant effort to keep them in step. That was the deciding argument against it for years. Shopify’s case is that agents have shrunk it, and they’re careful to say the cost hasn’t disappeared, only that it’s no longer decisive for them. With hundreds of mobile engineers and their own tooling, I can see it. I’m not convinced the maths has shifted yet for a small team.

The hybrid middle

I’ve worked on hybrids too, which means native for the parts that need the performance and cross-platform for the presentation. It’s a sensible answer to the “which one” question and the one nobody writes hot takes about.

The usual shape is a native app with React Native screens embedded in it. Shopify took this brownfield route for some of its biggest apps when it adopted RN, because a rewrite would have taken years and frozen feature work. You move screen by screen and keep shipping.

The cost is the seam. Two toolchains, navigation that crosses the boundary, state shared between two runtimes, and debugging that jumps languages. The team needs both skill sets, and ownership of the boundary gets fuzzy. I’ve experienced this too, and fuzzy boundaries lead to decision paralysis.

Discord’s version is different in an interesting way. On mobile, it keeps a native interface and shares app code with desktop through Rust. RN shares the UI layer. Discord shares the logic and leaves the UI native. Shopify’s new architecture points the same direction, for different reasons: business logic is decoupled from the UI so it can run headlessly. Two companies leaving RN landed on a similar principle, which is to keep the logic independent of whatever draws the screen.

That’s what appeals to me about hybrids. Nobody has to bet the whole company on one choice, and a stack decision becomes something you can revise one screen at a time.

Decision checklist

Before you pick a stack, or decide whether to leave one, here are the questions I’d want answered. None of them has a universally right answer, which is the point.

  1. Who is going to build and maintain this? Team size, existing skills and the hiring pool all matter. A team of web engineers and a team of Swift specialists will make different stacks look cheap or expensive.
  2. What does the app actually do? Most apps are lists, forms and network calls. Some lean on widgets, watch apps, background work, camera pipelines or heavy animation. The more platform-specific surface you have, the more a cross-platform layer has to bridge.
  3. How quickly do you need new OS features? If the product depends on being first with what Apple or Google ship, count the cost of writing and maintaining those native modules yourself.
  4. Have you measured? If the argument for switching is performance, profile the app you have and find out whether the framework is the bottleneck. A belief isn’t a benchmark.
  5. Who maintains the libraries you can’t live without? Shopify’s own announcement shows how this can change. FlashList needs a new steward and Skia is being forked under a new name. Check how exposed you are before it becomes your problem.
  6. Where does the product need to be in a year or two? Choose for the product you’re building towards, not the one you shipped last quarter. A prototype and a platform-feature-heavy flagship need different foundations.
  7. What does parity cost you, and has that changed? Agents may have lowered the cost of building twice. Shopify says so, and also says the cost hasn’t gone away. Try it on a real feature in your own codebase before taking anyone’s blog post on trust, mine included.
  8. How hard is it to change your mind? This calls back to The Retroactive Sorry: is this a two-way door? A hybrid or screen-by-screen approach keeps it more open than a big-bang decision.
  9. Is the current choice still ticking the boxes? This applies to existing apps, not just new ones. Write down why you picked your stack, then check whether those reasons still hold.

The last one is the one I’d put on a calendar. Shopify didn’t leave React Native because it stopped working. They left because an assumption based on a previous decision changed, and they went back to validate it.

Side note: Sometimes the decision simply isn’t yours or the team’s to make, but that doesn’t stop you doing your due diligence and pushing back as a tech leader.

Where I land

React Native is a good tool, native is a good tool, and a hybrid is a good tool. Each has been the right call for some product I’ve worked on and the wrong call for another.

Right now I have an indie app I’m building, and React Native makes the most sense to me. I’m building iOS first, and it leaves the door open for me to simply publish Android later with some fixes. At the time I was picking the architecture, Exact dropped from Expo. It’s a cross-platform framework (web, macOS, iOS and Linux, but no Android yet) built to be agent-first, meaning designed for AI agents as well as people to build with, and with its own declarative UI language, “Contract”.

I did consider jumping on it, but the framework is pre-1.0 and extremely new, so I decided it wasn’t the right choice for now. I’ll keep an eye on it as it matures. React Native is still on 0.x version numbers too, but over a decade of production use says more than a version number does.

So when Shopify or Discord announces it’s leaving something, I read the reasoning and ignore the logo. Shopify’s reasons come from hundreds of mobile engineers, a huge number of screens, and a purpose-built agent pipeline. Discord’s come from a wholesale move to Rust, with performance and memory gains the likely draw. If your situation doesn’t look like theirs, their conclusion doesn’t transfer, however many contributors they have.

What does transfer is the habit. Know where your product needs to go, check honestly whether your current stack gets you there, and look again when something underneath the decision changes. That could be a new model, a new OS or a library losing its maintainer. It could also be nothing, and in that case you carry on and ship the thing.
